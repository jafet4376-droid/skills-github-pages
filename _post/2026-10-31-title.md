Spintronics: the future of electronics beyond charge




















10








Spintronics (spin electronics) is a field of physics and engineering that exploits not only the electrical charge of electrons, but also their intrinsic angular momentum, known as spin, to store, process, and transmit information.

It is particularly interesting because conventional electronics primarily manipulate electron charge, whereas spintronics introduces another degree of freedom that can be exploited for computing and memory.
1. The fundamental idea
An electron has two fundamental properties relevant here:
- Electric charge: allows it to participate in electrical currents.
- Spin: an intrinsic quantum-mechanical property associated with angular momentum and a magnetic moment.
For simplified explanations, spin is often represented by two states:
Spin up (↑)

One spin orientation


Spin down (↓)

Opposite spin orientation



These states can be used to encode information, much like 0 and 1 in conventional computing.

Importantly, spin is not literally an electron rotating like a tiny ball. It is a quantum property.
2. How does it work?
The central challenge is learning how to generate, transport, manipulate and detect electron spin.

Three important mechanisms are:
A. Giant magnetoresistance (GMR)







tsymbal.unl.edu


Discovered in 1988 by Albert Fert and Peter Grünberg.

Imagine two magnetic layers separated by a nonmagnetic conductive layer.
- When their magnetizations are parallel, electrical resistance is relatively low.
- When they are antiparallel, resistance increases.
This makes it possible to detect magnetic configurations electrically.

It revolutionized hard disk read-head technology and earned Fert and Grünberg the 2007 Nobel Prize in Physics. 
IEEE Technology Navigator+1

B. Tunnel magnetoresistance (TMR)







slideserve.com


A magnetic tunnel junction (MTJ) consists of:
1. A fixed magnetic layer.
2. An extremely thin insulating barrier.
3. A free magnetic layer.
Electrons tunnel through the insulating barrier. The electrical resistance depends on the relative magnetization of the two magnetic layers.

This is one of the fundamental technologies behind MRAM.
C. Spin-transfer torque (STT)







mdpi.com


An electric current containing spin-polarized electrons can transfer angular momentum to a magnetic layer, changing its magnetization.

Essentially, we can use electrical currents to manipulate magnetic states.

This is particularly important for writing information into spintronic memory devices.
3. Where is spintronics being used?









MRAM — Magnetic Random Access Memory

Nonvolatile memory that retains information without continuous power. It is already commercially deployed and can be integrated into electronic systems.












Data storage

GMR and TMR technologies enable extremely sensitive magnetic read heads in hard disk drives.












Neuromorphic computing

Researchers are exploring magnetic dynamics to emulate certain characteristics of biological neurons and build unconventional computing architectures.












Magnetic sensors

Applications include automotive electronics, industrial positioning, robotics and biomedical instrumentation.




PMC+2
4. Why is it important for the future of computing?
One of the biggest problems in modern computing is energy consumption.

Conventional CMOS transistors use electrical charge to represent and manipulate information. They also experience energy losses from leakage and switching.

Spintronics offers some potentially interesting advantages:
Property	Potential advantage
Nonvolatility	Retains information without continuous power
Energy efficiency	Potentially lower-energy switching
Integration	Can combine memory and processing
Density	Potential for highly compact devices
Neuromorphic computing	Exploits magnetic dynamics for unconventional computation

However, there is an important distinction: spintronic memory is commercially established, while general-purpose spintronic logic is still an active research area. It has not yet demonstrated a complete processor that outperforms conventional CMOS systems. 
Nature Reviews Electrical Engineering+1
